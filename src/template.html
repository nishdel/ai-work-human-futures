Method, assumptions & sources

What the simulator actually does

It is a transparent stress-test model, not a scientific forecast or a claim of inevitability.

Four layers per domain

  • Cognitive capability: can AI reason, plan, perceive, and use tools?
  • Reliability: can it recover from errors and sustain long tasks at acceptable failure rates?
  • Embodiment: can hardware act safely in messy physical environments?
  • Adoption: do cost, regulation, liability, integration, trust, and demand allow deployment?

Uncertainty model

Technology updates use 240 seeded Monte Carlo paths; cached results are reused. It varies global progress regimes, domain-specific capability dates, robotics, reliability, adoption, and ceiling estimates. The 10–90% bands are uncertainty inside this model only; they do not capture every unknown.

Dates after 2030 are especially sensitive to assumptions about scaling limits, AI-assisted R&D, hardware deployment, and institutional response.

Definitions

  • Technical substitutability: share of current tasks machines could perform under favorable deployment.
  • Actual substitution: technical capability multiplied by integration, regulation, trust, and human-preference constraints.
  • Net human labor demand: unautomated current tasks plus modeled new human tasks.
  • Post-labor-like: below 25% of 2026 human hours—not zero work and not zero purpose.

Major omissions

The simulator does not explicitly model wars, pandemics, climate shocks, national divergence, demographic change, model theft, radical new architectures, or a single catastrophic discontinuity. Those can dominate the result. It also uses illustrative global work-hour weights rather than an official employment forecast.
